# Script 05 — "What If You Could Talk to Animals?"

**Format:** 16:9 YouTube, 1920 × 1080, 24 fps, about 8¾ minutes (~1,240 words at ~140 words/min).
**Style:** 2D studio, the channel's hero (blue hoodie). Follows `CLAUDE.md` and the fun → curse structure in
`docs/14-fun-to-curse-structure.md`.
**Status:** draft v1 (Oct 21), waiting for the user's approval before any voicing.

## The rule of the story
- One morning you understand every animal, and they understand you.
- There's no off switch. Not for a second. (This is the rule that turns the fun into the curse.)
- Animals don't do polite. They say exactly what they mean.

| Project CETI scientists | white T-shirts, "PROJECT CETI" as plain text, no logo | no lines |

**Recurring elements:**
- Animal speech shown as small speech bubbles that pop next to the animal (the hero hears it; nobody else does).
- The **4:47 a.m.** bedside clock (comic in Act 2, eerie in the twist).
- Biscuit's **sunny spot** on the living-room floor.
- "41" pieces of bacon counter (callback).
- REAL fact boxes in the science part. Project CETI only as plain text, no logo.
- End card: "What would YOU do?", subscribe, PART 2.

## The voiceover script
Speaker tags: unmarked lines are the NARRATOR. `BISCUIT:`, `PICKLES:` etc. are character lines.

```
[ACT 1 — HOOK]
BISCUIT: Bacon. Under the chair. Left side.
You look. There's a piece of bacon under the chair. Left side.
BISCUIT: That's forty-one pieces this year. I've been counting.
The cat walks across the breakfast table.
PICKLES: Ugh. You're awake.
And outside, every bird on the street is screaming the same thing.
BIRDS: My tree! My tree! MY TREE!
So... what if you could talk to animals?

[ACT 1 — RULES]
Here's how it works. You understand every animal. And every animal understands you.
There's no off switch. Not for a second. That part matters later.
Biscuit is fourteen. He's a year older than you.
Mom found him at a gas station in the rain, the winter before you were born.
A wet, angry puppy who bit her sleeve and wouldn't let go.
He's been there for every day of your life. Every scraped knee. Every bad dream.
You've talked to him every single day.
Today, for the first time, he talks back.

[ACT 2 — THE FUN PART]
And at first, it's the best thing that has ever happened to you.
You ask Biscuit what he wants for his birthday.
BISCUIT: The couch. All of it. Forever.
You ask Pickles why she keeps pushing cups off the table.
PICKLES: To check if they still fall.
A pigeon gives you directions to school.
PIGEON: Left at the bakery. Right at the man with the sandwich. Stay near the man with the sandwich.
A squirrel finds the house key you lost in May. He wants a peanut. Then he wants forty peanuts.
You start leaving peanuts out for the crows, and the crows start leaving you presents. A button. A bottle cap. A little piece of blue glass.
That really happens. A girl in Seattle got crow presents for years.
On the school trip to the zoo, the prairie dogs see you coming.
PRAIRIE DOG: Tall one. Blue top. Walks funny.
Theo films you talking to a pigeon. The video gets two million views.
The comments are full of people asking what their cat really thinks of them.
You know. You're not telling them.

[ACT 3 — THE CURSE]
Then, at 4:47 in the morning, the birds wake you up.
BIRDS: My tree! MY TREE!
That's not a joke, by the way. Most birdsong at dawn really means: this is my patch, stay out.
You can't sleep through it. You can't switch it off.
The mosquito in your room says nothing. That whine is just its wings, beating hundreds of times a second.
Somehow, that's worse.
Then you make a mistake. A crow steals your sandwich, and you call him ugly.
The next morning, there are twenty crows on your fence.
The morning after, a hundred. They dive at your head all the way to school.
Crows remember faces for years.
Scientists in Seattle once caught a few crows while wearing a caveman mask.
Years later, crows that were never caught still screamed at that mask. The others had taught them.
Now every crow in the city knows your face.
So you keep your hood up. All the time.
At school, people start calling you Squirrel Kid.
You sit alone at lunch, and a pigeon sits next to you. It's not the same.
And then there's the pet shop.
You walk in for cat food, and the whole room turns to you at once.
Every puppy. Every rabbit. Every bird in every cage.
ANIMALS: Pick me. Pick me. Take me home.
You can't take them all. You can't even take one.
You walk out with a bag of cat food, and you don't look back.
MOM: Hey. What's wrong?
You say nothing. Biscuit doesn't either.
But that night, he sleeps across your doorway.

[ACT 4 — IS ANY OF THIS REAL?]
So... could animals really talk?
In a way, they already do.
Remember the prairie dogs? That's real.
A biologist in Arizona spent years recording their alarm calls.
They don't just shout "danger". They describe what's coming: a person, how tall, even the colour of their shirt.
Vervet monkeys have different alarms for a leopard, an eagle and a snake.
Hear "eagle", and they look up. Hear "snake", and they look down.
Honeybees dance to tell each other which way the flowers are, and how far.
That dance won a Nobel Prize in 1973.
And in 2024, scientists in Kenya found that wild elephants have names too.
Play an elephant a call meant for her, and she calls back. Meant for someone else? She mostly ignores it.
And here's one the internet gets wrong: one dog year isn't seven human years.
A 2020 study of Labradors' DNA worked out that a fourteen-year-old dog is about seventy-three.
Biscuit is seventy-three.
Right now, a team called Project CETI is using AI to decode the clicks of sperm whales.
They've already found patterns in them, like an alphabet.
None of these animals talk the way we do. But every one of them is saying something.
We just never learned how to listen.
Remember the whales. You're going to hear them again.

[ACT 5 — BISCUIT]
A week later, an email arrives. Project CETI has seen your video.
They send you a recording.
You put on the headphones. Clicks. Long, slow clicks.
And under the clicks, words. A mother whale calling her calf. The same name, over and over.
You write it down. They don't believe you.
Then they check their tapes. Every time she calls that name, the calf answers.
The news calls you the kid who talks to whales.
But that night, Biscuit doesn't come up the stairs.
You find him at the bottom, looking up at you.
BISCUIT: It's my back legs. They do this now.
You carry him up. He's lighter than he used to be.
BISCUIT: Don't tell your mom. She'll worry.
He's been hurting since the winter. He just couldn't tell anyone. Until now.
You tell Mom. The vet is kind. His hips are worn out, and his heart is tired.
Weeks. Maybe less.
So you spend every one of them together.
He tells you he's hated the vacuum cleaner since day one.
That he can hear Mom's car two streets away. That's why he's always at the door first.
BISCUIT: The day you were born, everybody left in a hurry. I waited by the door for two days.
BISCUIT: Then they came back with you. You were very small and very loud. I liked you right away.
On the last night, he lies in his sunny spot, even though it's dark.
BISCUIT: You talked to me every day of your life. I always understood.
BISCUIT: This year, you understood me back. Best year.
BISCUIT: Don't be sad for too long. Okay?
You say okay. It's the first time you ever lied to him.
He falls asleep with his head on your foot. And he doesn't wake up.
For weeks, you can't go to the park.
Every dog there says hello. None of them sound like him.
And there's still no off switch.

[ACT 5 — THE TWIST]
Then one morning, at 4:47, you wake up, and something's wrong.
It's quiet.
No birds. No "my tree". Not one crow on the fence.
Pickles is sitting at the window, ears flat, staring toward the sea.
Then you hear it. Not from the garden. From very, very far away.
Deeper than any voice you've ever heard. Thousands of them. Saying the same three words.
WHALES: We hear you.
Pickles doesn't turn around.
PICKLES: They've been asking for you all night.

[END]
So, if you could talk to animals, who would you talk to first, and what would you ask?
Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because the whales aren't done talking.
```

## Fact notes (checked)
- **Crow gifts:** Gabi Mann, Seattle, fed the crows in her garden from 2011; they brought her small objects (buttons,
  beads, bits of glass, a pearl-coloured heart). Reported by the BBC in 2015.
- **Prairie dogs:** Con Slobodchikoff (Northern Arizona University) found Gunnison's prairie dog alarm calls encode
  the type of predator, a human's size and shape, and the colour of their clothing (e.g. *Animal Cognition*, 2009).
- **Dawn chorus:** much of birdsong at dawn is territorial defence ("this is mine") and mate attraction.
- **Mosquito whine:** the sound is the wing beat, several hundred beats per second.
- **Crows and faces:** John Marzluff (University of Washington) trapped crows wearing a "caveman" mask (2006); years
  later crows that were never trapped, including young ones, scolded that mask: they learned it from other crows.
- **Vervet monkeys:** distinct alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, each with its own escape (Seyfarth, Cheney
  & Marler, 1980).
- **Honeybees:** the waggle dance's angle gives the direction relative to the sun, its duration the distance. Karl von
  Frisch shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
- **Elephants:** wild African savanna elephants in Kenya address each other with name-like calls and respond more to
  calls addressed to them (Pardo et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2024).
- **Dog years:** Wang et al. (*Cell Systems*, 2020, UC San Diego) used DNA methylation in Labrador retrievers:
  human age ≈ 16 · ln(dog age) + 31. For 14 years: 16 × 2.64 + 31 ≈ 73.
- **Project CETI:** a non-profit using machine learning to decode sperm whale codas (founded 2020; 2024 study of a
  "phonetic alphabet" of codas, *Nature Communications*). Shown as plain text only.
- **Story, not fact:** whales asking for you, a whale calf's "name", and animals speaking English are the story's
  power. The science part never claims them.
