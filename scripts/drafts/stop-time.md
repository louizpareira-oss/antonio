## The rule of the story
- Snap your fingers and the whole world stops. Snap again and it starts.
- **The world stops. You don't.** Every second you spend in the stop, you live, and you age.

## The voiceover script
Speaker tags: unmarked lines are the NARRATOR. `MIA:` is Mia.

```
[ACT 1 — HOOK]
Can one person really stop time?
Not in a movie. With real physics. Let's find out.
There's a raindrop hanging in front of your eye. You can see yourself in it.
It isn't falling. Nothing is.
You snapped your fingers. And the whole world stopped.
Birds in mid-air. Rain, hanging like glitter.
Only one thing is still moving. Grandpa Leo's old pocket watch. Tick. Tick.
Here's the rule. The world stops. You don't.
Every second in the stop, you live. And you age.
Remember that.

[ACT 1 — MIA]
This is Mia. Your twin. Born four minutes before you.
MIA: Four minutes. That's what respect looks like.
Last spring, she lost the state final by one hundredth of a second.
She still trains every morning at six. In the rain.

[ACT 2 — THE FUN PART]
Day one. Your alarm rings. Snap. You sleep three more hours. Nobody notices.
Math homework? Snap. Done, in zero seconds.
Your phone slips off the bus seat. Snap. You catch it an inch from the floor.
At the concert, you walk right to the front row.

[ACT 3 — THE CURSE]
But the world stops. You don't.
In the stop, the air doesn't move out of your way. Walking feels like pushing through honey.
And the longer you stay, the darker it gets.
And you keep living. Hours, in every snap.
By the end of the month, you're four months older than your birthday.
MIA: Did you get taller? Since breakfast?
Then the world notices. Every snap leaves a stutter. Songs skip.
NIST, the lab that keeps America's official time, sees its clocks disagree, centred on your town.
NASA sees it too.
Lasers bounced off the mirrors Apollo left on the Moon come back millimetres wrong.
Something is pulling at time. It's you.

[ACT 4 — IS ANY OF THIS REAL?]
So. Is any of this real?
Time really does change speed. In 1971, scientists flew atomic clocks around the world on airliners.
When they landed, they disagreed with the clocks on the ground. By billionths of a second, as Einstein predicted.
Astronaut Scott Kelly spent almost a year in space. He came back a few thousandths of a second younger than his twin.
At Harvard, scientists slowed light to bicycle speed. Then they stopped it, and let it go.
Near a black hole, a falling clock seems to freeze.
But a real stop would trap you. The air wouldn't move. No light would reach your eyes.
Stopping time is real. Just never for the whole world. And never for free.

[ACT 5 — THE CLIMAX]
Six a.m. Rain. Mia is sprinting across the crossing.
And a truck is sliding, wheels locked, straight at her.
Snap.
The truck is one metre from her. Frozen.
You can't push it. You can't lift her. The air is like concrete.
So you dig. Inch by inch, you move her out of the way.
It takes days. Then weeks. Then years.
Snap.
The truck hits nothing. Mia blinks. She stares at you.
MIA: Who are you?
Your sleeves are too short. You're seventeen. She's thirteen.
You used to be four minutes apart. Now it's four years.

[ACT 5 — THE TWIST]
Then she looks at her stopwatch. And smiles.
MIA: Four years. That's nothing.
MIA: Who do you think stopped the world the first time, at that state final?
MIA: I just lost the race on purpose.
Her stopwatch is still ticking. The world isn't.
The raindrop hangs in front of your eye again.
And this time, you didn't snap.

[END]
If you could stop the world for one hour, what would you do?
Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because Mia isn't the only one.
```

## Fact notes (to check before voicing)
- **Hafele–Keating, 1971:** caesium atomic clocks flown around the world eastward and westward on commercial airliners;
  compared with the US Naval Observatory clocks they differed by about −59 ns (east) and +273 ns (west), in line with
  special and general relativity.
- **Scott Kelly:** about 340 days on the International Space Station (2015–16), NASA Twins Study with his twin Mark.
  Special-relativistic time dilation from orbital speed outweighs the gravitational effect in low orbit, so he aged
  roughly 5 ms less than if he had stayed on Earth ("a few thousandths of a second").
- **Light slowed and stopped:** Lene Hau's group at Harvard slowed light to about 17 m/s in an ultracold sodium cloud
  (*Nature*, 1999) and in 2001 stopped a light pulse and released it again. (Bicycle speed ≈ 17 m/s ≈ 61 km/h is a fast
  bike; keep "bicycle speed" or say "the speed of a car in town".)
- **Black holes:** to a distant observer, a clock falling toward the event horizon appears to slow down and freeze
  (gravitational time dilation and redshift).
- **A real time stop:** if only you moved, air molecules would not move out of your way (like a wall) and no photons
  would arrive at your eyes (darkness); a classic thought experiment.
- **NIST:** keeps the official time of the United States (atomic clocks in Boulder, Colorado). **Lunar laser ranging:**
  observatories bounce lasers off the retroreflectors left by Apollo 11, 14 and 15 and measure the Earth–Moon
  distance to about a millimetre.
- **Story, not fact:** stopping time, the stutter, NIST's clocks disagreeing over your town, the laser ranging error,
  moving through frozen air for years, Mia's power.
