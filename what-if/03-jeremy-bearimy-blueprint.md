## 2. Why it works

1. **The viewer is the protagonist from the first sentence.** Descriptions open in second person and present tense, in the middle of a scene, with no intro or channel plug. Examples: "Everything goes black—and suddenly, you're back in your bedroom" (Respawn) and "You walk into your new classroom, and every conversation stops" (Girls Only School). [S]
2. **Every power is shown as a visible game system.** Examples: a glowing heart above someone's head (Crush), an item menu that puts things in your hands (Creative Mode), a "BUZZER" label chip [M] and a "78 BPM" meter [U]. One frame is enough to understand the premise, which is what a thumbnail and the first 20 seconds need. [S][M][U]
3. **Romance and social status are the breakout lane.** The only 1M+ video is a crush and social-power premise, at about 1M in about 1 day. The body premises sat near 300K at the earlier snapshot. [S]
4. **The cost is emotional, and viewers argue about the rules.** Respawn commenters discuss the guilt of keeping your memories, and ask where the checkpoint is and how many lives are left. The audience debates the rule set, and that debate fills the comments. [S]
5. **The stakes are personal, not planet-sized.** Science "What If" channels (for example @whatifscienceshow, 8.99M subscribers per a search snippet) explain in third person with planet-sized stakes. This channel is about you, your school and your crush, and it runs on game logic. Second person alone is not the edge: a 2026 wave of "What If You…" channels also uses it ("100x Smarter", "Kindergarten With All Your Memories"). [S] for the examples, [I] for the reading
6. **They ship fast, follow up quickly and moved toward what worked.**
   - At least four uploads between Oct 22 and ~Nov 3, or five if the Sleepover video is theirs.
   - Girls Only School (Oct 29) was followed by Girls Only Sleepover (~Nov 4).
   - OutlierKit described the early catalog as "speculative human biology and perception scenarios". The newer titles are game, power and romance premises.

   [S] for the dates and descriptions, [!] on the Sleepover attribution, [I] on the pivot
7. **A cheap 3D kit with one consistent mood.**
   - Sets are built from primitives, crowds are colour-swapped copies of one rig, and every character uses one skin material and decal faces.
   - Every scene is graded violet-indigo with warm practical lights, and mean luma is only 68–95/255.
   - The result is many scenes that all share one look.

   [M]; the cost point is [I]
8. **Growth comes in spikes.** One premise can outperform the whole back catalog (see §1). So how each premise is packaged (title, thumbnail, first 20 seconds) matters more than how many videos go out. [S] for the numbers, [I] for the conclusion

### Topic rules for your channel [I]
1. Take a desire teens already have (be liked, be noticed, win, skip effort) and grant it as a rule.
2. The power must be drawable as one object or HUD element. This is the thumbnail test.
3. The rule that grants the power must later take something away. This is the planted cost.
4. Use everyday settings: bedroom, classroom, gym, sleepover, kitchen.
5. Make it sequel-able. If a video beats your baseline, ship a variant within a week.
6. Skip tragedy premises (their #12 is about a school shooting). They carry a risk with advertisers and with audience trust.

## 4. The 10-minute script template

**What this is based on:**
- Crush runs 10:53. [S]
- The openings of six video descriptions. [S]
- The comments on Respawn. [S]

No narration could be measured because no transcript was reachable. The timings below are a template, not a measurement. Word counts assume **150 words per minute**, which is also an assumption: 10:00 is about 1,500 words, and a 10:53 cut is about 1,630.

### Timed beat sheet

| Time | Beat | Words | What happens | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | 1. Cold open | 50 | Say the title question once, then drop into a you/present-tense scene where the power is already working. The first image matches the thumbnail. | [S] |
| 0:20–1:00 | 2. Rules | 100 | State 2–4 rules like game mechanics: the trigger, what you see, and one "but". Plant the hidden cost in a throwaway line. The HUD label and meter appear. | [S] the second sentence carries the rule or catch |
| 1:00–1:45 | 3. First test (private) | 110 | Try it alone at home. A small win and a small gag. | [I] |
| 1:45–2:30 | 4. Public debut | 110 | Use it at school. The first social payoff, with a deadpan reaction close-up. | [I] |
| 2:30–3:30 | 5. Peak fun | 150 | A montage of three wish-fulfilment scenes, each ending on a punchline. | [I] |
| 3:30–4:30 | 6. First crack | 150 | The planted rule bites, lightly. Someone notices. | [I] |
| 4:30–5:30 | 7. Midpoint turn | 150 | A misunderstanding or a secret to keep. The meter starts moving the wrong way. | [I] |
| 5:30–6:30 | 8. Spiral | 150 | Using the power to fix the problem makes it worse. | [I] |
| 6:30–8:30 | 9. Low point | 300 | The emotional cost: guilt, a lost friend or crush, losing yourself. Slower cuts and quieter music. | [S] the Respawn comments point to guilt; [I] for the placement |
| 8:30–9:40 | 10. Choice or twist | 180 | Keep the power or give it up, or a twist that pays off the planted rule. | [I] |
| 9:40–10:00 | 11. Outro | 50 | "Be honest — would you X or Y?" plus a sequel tease. | [S], from "What IF" videos whose channel is unconfirmed [!] |
| | **Total** | **1,500** | | |

**Pacing [I]:** change the beat, the location or the HUD every 30–45 seconds, which gives 12–16 changes per video. To run 10:53 like Crush, add about 130 words across beats 5 and 9.

### Worked example: #9 "What If You Had a Minimap in Real Life?" [I]
1. You wake up, and a glowing circle in the corner of your vision shows your room from above.
2. Green dots are friends, red dots are teachers and the gold dot is your crush. But the map shows where people are, never how they feel.
3. You find the TV remote and dodge your sister in the hallway.
4. At school you never get caught, and you keep "accidentally" running into the gold dot.
5. You become a hide-and-seek legend, you are never late, and you know every shortcut in town.
6. Your best friend's dot sits alone behind the gym every lunch. You're busy.
7. Your crush asks why you're always there. She thinks you're following her.
8. You zoom out to fix it and track everyone, and you lose your friend's dot completely.
9. You find him without the map. He has been having a hard week, and you missed it because you were watching dots, not faces.
10. You close the map, or set it to show only one dot.
11. "Be honest — would you keep the map, or turn it off?" Tease the sequel: what if the map showed who is looking for you?

### Narration rules
1. Always "you", always present tense. [S]
2. Say the title question once, then go straight into the scene, with no intro and no subscribe request at the start. [S]
3. The second sentence gives the rule or the catch, often with "but". [S]
4. Write short declarative sentences. Reveals land on an em-dash or "and suddenly". [S]
5. Use ordinary settings: bedroom, classroom, sleepover, gym. [S][M]
6. Turn the power into a game system: menus, checkpoints, lives, HUD. [S]
7. Fun first, cost second. Social and emotional costs work better than gore. [I], backed by the Respawn comments [S]
8. Keep it PG and relatable for teens, with humour that comes from awkwardness (a class going silent when you walk in). [S]
9. Leave one rule open on purpose. Viewers asked about checkpoints and lives, so give them something to argue about. [I], from the comments [S]
10. Let characters land 1–3-word punchlines that get their own caption card. [M]

### Endings and calls to action
- **Binary question [S][!]:** "Be honest… X or Y?" Examples: "join Project IF or fight them?", "take the chocolate or leave the money?", "tell your best friend or keep it secret?". These appear on "What IF" videos from the same weeks, but those videos are **not confirmed** as this channel's.
- **Sequel when a premise works [S][!]:** a successful premise gets a variant within about a week, as Girls Only School led to Girls Only Sleepover (the Sleepover's attribution is unconfirmed).
- **Possible running storyline [S][!]:** "Project IF" and "back to the IF series" appear on the same unconfirmed group of videos. Don't assume they belong to this channel.
- **Description [S]:** it opens with the title question, then one second-person scene line. Crush uses a heart emoji, then "You walk into a room, and a glowing heart appears above someone's head."
- **Unknown:** chapters, pinned comments and end screens.

### 5.3 Lighting, materials and outlines [M]

**Lighting and grade**
- The key light is warm peach and the shadows are cool violet or navy.
- There is a strong rim light on the key side. It reaches #fffeff on the head and stays bright even on a 46 px distant head.
- Exposure: mean luma 68–95/255, median 47–85, 95th percentile 130–174. Mean saturation is 31–46%.
- Every scene is graded toward violet and indigo.
- Atmospheric haze lifts distant values: the court reads #6f455a near the camera and #9a717e far away.
- There is no uniform vignette. Corners darken only where the set lighting does.
- Lit props carry the mood: string lights, a floodlight with heavy bloom, red alarm-clock digits, and a slit of light from a door or window.
- Bloom, depth of field and volumetric haze are used throughout.

**Materials**
- There are no textures anywhere. Each surface is one flat colour with low specular.

**Outlines**
- Colour: dark indigo, #231b40–#2f1b40.
- Width: about 0.25% of frame width, or about 4–5 px at 1080p.
- The width is the same on a 235 px head and a 46 px head. That means it is a constant-width screen-space line, not an inverted-hull outline that scales with the object.
- It follows silhouettes plus some interior edges, such as sleeve against hand and strands of hair.

  - glowing lines or particles inside the scene show the power itself.

### 5.7 Subtitles [M] (these may be the re-uploader's)
- **Style:** white #ffffff in a regular-weight humanist sans (Source Sans or Noto-like). No box, no stroke, a faint shadow. Mostly lowercase or sentence case.
- **Size and position:** 5.4–6.4% of frame height, about 60–70 px at 1080p. Centred, with the baseline at 92–95% of frame height.
- **Chunking:** 3–7 words per card ("pick up basketball after school"). Dialogue punchlines get their own card ("bro how").
- **Tool:** the bilingual layout looks like CapCut or Jianying auto-captions. [I]

### 5.8 Thumbnail formula: not observed
No thumbnail could be downloaded, so there is no measured formula.

**Starting points to test [I].** These are not taken from their thumbnails.
- One character, with the head at 40–50% of thumbnail height and a big readable expression.
- The premise's mechanic shown as a single object or HUD element.
- 0–3 words of text.
- A hot rim light against a dark single-hue background.
- Exposure lifted well above the in-video grade, which has a mean luma of only 68–95.

**What to record once their thumbnails are reachable:**
- number of characters
- head size as a percentage of height
- text: word count, case, font, colour
- background hue
- expression
- whether a HUD element is present
- whether the layout repeats across videos

### 5.9 Likely tooling [I]
- **Signs of a real 3D pipeline:** constant-width screen-space outlines, instanced colour-swapped crowds, geometrically clean nets, arcs and light strings, and bloom, depth of field and haze.
- **Most plausible solo stack:** Blender EEVEE with Grease Pencil Line Art or Freestyle for the outlines. Unity or Unreal with a toon post-process would also fit.
- **AI:** the stills show no AI artefacts; decals are stable and repeated geometry is consistent. That does not rule out AI in motion.

## 6. Production pipeline: one video a week

**What the research points to.** The target appears to use:
- a 3D kit built from primitives, with screen-space outlines (Blender EEVEE with Line Art is the closest match);

- one HUD device per premise;
- CapCut-style captions.

It ships roughly every 3–5 days. **All the hours below are planning estimates for a solo creator, not research findings.** [I]

## 7. Make it yours: Mustard

**Already decided (keep it).** Mustard is a ball-head character in a mustard-yellow hoodie and navy pants, with the same 3D look.
- A sphere head on a primitive body is a genre convention. The target does not own it.
- Mustard's colours already set him apart from their likely "you" character, a bald white head in a blue hoodie (#3b57a0–#4b6bbe; inferred from three frames).
- Their character also wears a hoodie with a hood and kangaroo pocket [M], so colour is currently the main difference.
- The job is to make everything around Mustard clearly yours. [I]

**The name and the "you" narration [I].** Keep the narration in second person, because that is what drives the format. Let Mustard be the on-screen stand-in for "you", the way their blue-hoodie character is. Use the name "Mustard" in branding, thumbnails, community posts and recurring lore, not in the narration.

### Keep: genre mechanics nobody owns
- Second-person, present-tense cold openings; a fun, then cost, then choice arc; a binary question at the end.
- One visible device per premise (a label, a meter, an effect inside the scene).
- Primitive 3D characters with decal faces and big readable expressions, plus one reusable crowd rig.
- A mood built on practical lights, short caption chunks, and reaction close-ups cut against crowd wides.
- Fast variants of whatever outperforms.
