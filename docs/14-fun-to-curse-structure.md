# The "fun part → curse" structure (analysis of 8 viral What-If transcripts, Oct 21)

The user shared 8 transcripts (invisible, walk through walls, eagle eyes, copy powers, teleport, money ×10 a day,
can't die, wake up in kindergarten). We don't copy their topics. This is what they do, so we can do it our own way.

---

## 1. The hook: a physical test in the first 30 seconds

Every one opens inside a body, in a place the viewer was in this week, and tests the power on something small.

| Video | Opening place | The test (small, physical, measurable) | First laugh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Invisible | packed train, "nobody looks at you" | your hands vanish, you see through yourself | "they never noticed you before either" |
| Walls | a brick wall | face first, 3 s of dark silence inside the wall | you step out in the neighbour's kitchen mid-dinner |
| Eagle eyes | classroom | read a friend's phone from 6 m ("normal eyes: 1 m") | you spot his typo |
| Copy powers | school cafeteria | a kid floats a spoon, your arm brushes his, your spoon floats | "Hey, that's my thing" |
| Teleport | late, bus pulling away, one shoe on | picture the back seat, blink | you land on someone; your other shoe is still on the porch |
| Money | cold open: day 15 | "more money than everyone on Earth, and the deli guy won't take it" | — (paradox instead of a laugh) |
| Can't die | a forest, a strange bottle | walk off a cliff, cut a finger, it heals | "common sense isn't your strongest quality" |
| Kindergarten | cold open: a sealed envelope, "NOVA REYES!" | tiny hands | — (proof instead of a laugh) |

What they all share:
- **Second person, present tense, an ordinary place.** Train, classroom, cafeteria, bus stop. The viewer is already
  there.
- **The first use of the power is tiny.** A spoon, a phone screen, a bus seat. Never the world on day one.
- **The test has a number the viewer can feel:** "a phone from 1 m, you read it from 6", "3 seconds inside the wall".
- **A laugh within ~10 s,** from a specific detail (the other shoe on the porch, the neighbour's dinner).
- **The title question lands at 10–20 s:** "So, what if you could…?"
- **The rule in one sentence, with a cost hidden inside it:** "hold your breath and you go through; breathe and
  you're solid", "your clothes don't vanish", "you keep your speed", "you copy the cost". Then they point at it:
  *"Remember that rule, because it's going to matter a lot."* / *"It is going to be so much weirder than you think."*
- **An emotional seed** that pays off in the last minute: "nobody looks at you" → "somebody saved you a seat";
  mom's $10 jar → "it's worth $10 again".
- Alternative opener (money, kindergarten): a **cold-open flash-forward** that shows the end state as a paradox, then
  rewinds.

## 2. The pivot: when the fun part turns into the curse

Rough positions (word counts):

| Video | Fun part | Pivot line | Pivot at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Invisible | skip lines, scare Juno | "nobody can see you coming either, so people walk right into you" | ~20 % |
| Money | days 3–5: coffee machine, pizza for the school, a car | "Welcome to the not so fun part." (day 6, the bank) | ~25 % |
| Walls | keys, ball over the fence, shortcut through the bakery | "But the first problem shows up fast." | ~27 % |
| Copy powers | collecting powers like a notebook | "The more you collect, the more problems you find." | ~40 % |
| Teleport | never miss the bus, homework in 11 s, a lake at lunch | "The more you use it, the more problems you find." | ~40 % |
| Eagle eyes (short) | answer sheet, archery, coin on a pitch | "But eagle eyes come with a price." | ~65 % |
| Can't die | heals, superhero, a century of fun | friends age; then the war takes a friend | ~20 % / 35 % |
| Kindergarten (short) | "Plus: … Plus: …" | almost none: "money attracts sharks" | ~70 % |

The pattern:
- **Fun part = 5–8 tiny wishes from the viewer's own day.** Each wish is one picture: never late, front of the line,
  the dog ate my homework. It's short: the first quarter of the video.
- **The pivot is said out loud,** in one plain line. Nobody hides it.
- **The curse climbs a ladder:** comic (people bump into you, the pop in the library) → physical danger (the car
  stops 30 cm away, Everest, stuck inside a stone block) → social (friends stop trusting you, nobody high-fives you,
  "she doesn't say it's your fault") → emotional (loneliness, forgetting her voice, forgetting where your body ends).
- **Every curse comes from the rule set in the hook.** The breath rule becomes the stone block; "keep your speed"
  becomes the North Pole; "copy the cost" becomes the mind-reader's headaches. No random new problems.
- **The last curse hits the person planted in the hook** (Juno, the sister, the best friend, mom's jar, the wife).
- **Then the hero uses the power for someone else,** with a skill the curse taught them: bathtub breathing → teaching
  the boy in the elevator to breathe 4-in, 4-out; the lifeguard's 10-second rule → the drowning boy.
- **Last 20–30 s: a twist that opens a sequel** (a room of photos with your face marked "next", the rescue team calls,
  "Bro, is this a glitch?"), then the comment question.

## 3. How the real science is woven in

- **Facts arrive as rules or problems, never as a lecture.** "Rule two. About those eyes. They're not a cartoon
  mistake. They're physics." Each fact is the reason the next scene happens.
- **Fact → consequence in the next sentence.** The ground spins at 1,670 km/h → you roll into the fence. LED bulbs
  blink 100 times a second → you wear sunglasses in the kitchen.
- **Every number is made human-sized:** an atom as a stadium with a marble in the middle; the bull's-eye is a sesame
  seed at arm's length; $10 million weighs 220 lb, more than you; one bowl of ramen in Tokyo = two weeks of jet lag.
- **Myth-busting earns trust:** "Not eight times better like the internet says. About five." "Most lottery winners
  go broke? That number was never real."
- **Specific place + year = credibility:** Innsbruck 1997, Parma 1990s, Halloween 1976, Hungary 1946, Zimbabwe
  2008, "off the coast of Indonesia in 1998".
- **Nature as proof the power half-exists:** glass frogs hide their red blood cells in the liver, the mimic octopus,
  the lyrebird.
- **They admit where fiction bends physics:** "In real life that's two dark dots. Here they're white. Close enough."
  This keeps the viewer on side.
- **Science also powers the emotional curse:** the left-out-of-catch experiment (loneliness), the rubber hand
  illusion (forgetting where you end), memories fade (forgetting her voice).
- Density: about one real fact every 20–40 s. Our rule stays: the bigger "Is any of this real?" beat goes in the
  **middle**, between Act 2 and Act 3, with smaller facts woven everywhere else.

## What keeps it from sounding like AI

- Short sentences. One picture per sentence, ~8–12 words, so a new scene every 3–4 s at ~140 wpm.
- Jokes come from specifics and understatement, never from "Imagine!": "Your mom thinks you started taking
  extremely long baths. She's a little worried." "The coffee machine has bodyguards."
- One named person with one small object that carries the feeling: Juno carrying the hoodie all week, mom's $10 jar,
  the sister's hand in the thunderstorm.
- Callbacks: the last scene repeats the first place (the train, the jar) with one thing changed.
- A moral choice, not just a disaster: would you still be good if nobody could see you?
- The hero keeps a scar: something the power took that doesn't come back.

## Template for an 8-minute Parallel You video

| Time | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Ordinary place, tiny physical test with a number, a laugh, the title question, the rule + "remember this rule" |
| 0:30–1:00 | How it would work (one real fact), the family member and the small object |
| 1:00–2:15 | Fun part: 6 tiny wishes, one picture each |
| ~2:15 | Pivot line, said plainly |
| 2:15–4:00 | Curse ladder: comic → danger (from the rule) |
| 4:00–4:45 | "Is any of this real?": 3–4 facts, myth-bust, nature's version |
| 4:45–6:30 | Social → emotional curse, hits the family member; the lasting price |
| 6:30–7:30 | Hero moment using a skill the curse taught; world-scale effect |
| 7:30–8:00 | Twist / sequel hook, "What would YOU do?" |

# 5 new ideas (none of the 8 topics, none of ours)

Hooks are written as beats, one scene per line (~3–4 s each). Facts are ones I'm confident in; anything marked
*(check)* gets verified before the script.

## A. What If You Turned Into a Human Magnet?
**Rule:** you're magnetic. Calm, you pull like a fridge magnet. The more scared or excited you get, the harder you
pull. *(The loaded gun: fear.)*
**Family:** little brother Leo, 5, born deaf. His cochlear implant is held on by a magnet behind his ear.

Hook:
1. 7:02 a.m. You open the cutlery drawer.
2. Every spoon slides to the front. Click. Click. Click.
3. The fridge magnets jump off the fridge and stick to your chest.
4. Leo's spoon floats out of his cereal toward you. The little disc behind his ear tilts.
5. "Do it again!"
6. You laugh. Your heart speeds up. The fork drawer flies open.
7. Every fork in the house is now on your hoodie.
8. So, what if you became a human magnet? And what happens when you get scared?

- **Fun:** your keys find you; euro cents jump to your hand (they're steel inside); Aunt Rita's "gold" chain jumps
  too (real gold isn't magnetic); 40 spoons on your face goes viral; you find every coin on the beach.
- **Pivot:** "Then, for the first time, you get scared."
- **Curse ladder:** comic: your bank card is wiped, every shopping cart follows you like ducklings → danger: a dog
  barks, you jump, a parked bike flies at you; the MRI room at Grandma's check-up → social: no airports, no
  laptops near you → emotional: you hug Leo and his sound processor snaps off his head onto your cheek. His world goes
  silent. He can't hear you say sorry. From now on you stay 2 m from your brother.
- **Real facts:** a fridge magnet is ~0.005 tesla, an MRI 1.5–3 T, Earth's field ~0.00005 T. In 2001, a 6-year-old
  boy died in New York when an MRI pulled a metal oxygen tank across the room. In 1997, Andre Geim levitated a live
  frog in a 16-tesla magnet (water is very weakly magnetic). Robins steer by Earth's field; in 2014, scientists in
  Oldenburg found city radio noise scrambles their compass. In 2019 the World Magnetic Model had to be updated a year
  early because magnetic north was moving so fast. Your body holds ~4 g of iron, but blood iron isn't magnetic.
- **Hero:** a delivery truck with no brakes rolls toward the crosswalk where Leo is. You stop trying to stay calm.
  You let yourself be terrified on purpose, and the truck stops a metre from him.
- **Twist:** that night every robin in town lands on your roof, all facing you. Morning news: magnetic north moved
  40 km… toward your town.
- **Comment question:** What's the first thing you'd pull toward you?

## B. What If You Became Cold-Blooded?
**Rule:** your body stops making its own heat. You're exactly as warm as the world around you. *(The loaded gun:
winter.)*
**Family:** Grandma, who knits you a new sweater every winter.

Hook:
1. You wake up and you can't move.
2. Not stuck. Just slow. Your fingers bend like cold honey.
3. Mom opens the curtains. "Bus. Five minutes."
4. A stripe of sun lands on your arm. Your arm wakes up first.
5. Then your shoulder. Then your face.
6. You didn't eat dinner last night. Or breakfast. You're not hungry.
7. It's July, 37 degrees outside, and you feel perfect.
8. So, what if you became cold-blooded? It's summer. For now.

- **Fun:** one meal every few days (lizards run on about a tenth of a mammal's food); free lunch money; on the
  hottest sports day you're the fastest kid there; mosquitoes barely find you (they home in on heat and breath);
  you nap in the sun like a cat and call it "charging".
- **Pivot:** "Then, on the first of September, the school turns on the air conditioning."
- **Curse ladder:** comic: you fall asleep mid-sentence in a 20-degree classroom; you freeze in the supermarket's
  freezer aisle → danger: a swim in a cold lake and your arms stop working halfway back → social: friends call you
  lazy, you live on the classroom windowsill → emotional: Grandma's sweater doesn't warm you anymore (a sweater only
  keeps in heat you make), and the doctor says that in winter you'll shut down until spring. Your sister's birthday,
  sledding with Dad, all of December, gone.
- **Real facts:** the wood frog lets up to two-thirds of its body water freeze, its heart stops, and in spring it
  thaws and hops away. Arctic ground squirrels cool to −2.9 °C in hibernation. Sick lizards give themselves a fever
  by basking (desert iguanas, 1975). NASA funded a study (SpaceWorks) on putting Mars astronauts into a torpor-like
  sleep to save food and space.
- **Hero / world-scale:** NASA calls. A cold-blooded crew would need a fraction of the food for a 3-year trip.
- **Twist:** you close your eyes in November. You open them. It's warm, too warm. "Good morning. You've been asleep
  seven months. We're halfway to Mars."
- **Comment question:** Would you trade every winter for never being hungry?

## C. What If You Could Live on Sunlight?
**Rule:** sunlight feeds you. Food doesn't anymore. *(The loaded gun: night, clouds, winter.)*
**Family:** Grandma, whose Sunday stew is how your family says "I love you".

Hook:
1. You skip breakfast. You forgot.
2. Lunch, too. You're not hungry.
3. 3 p.m. You wait for the bus in the sun.
4. Ten minutes later, you feel like you just ate a whole sandwich.
5. You look at your hands. Your fingertips are green.
6. Not marker. Green under the skin, like a leaf held up to the light.
7. A cloud passes, and your stomach growls.
8. So, what if you could live on sunlight?

- **Fun:** your lunch goes to your best friend; no dishes, no "finish your broccoli"; the window seat in exams is
  pure energy; at the black-light party you glow red (chlorophyll glows red under UV).
- **Pivot:** "Then it rains. For five days."
- **Curse ladder:** comic: every break you stand in the schoolyard with your arms out like a scarecrow; nights are
  long and hungry → danger: day four of rain, you faint; the hospital can't feed you through a tube, so they put you
  under grow lights → social: Sunday dinner, everyone eats Grandma's stew, you sit with an empty plate → emotional:
  winter in the north gives you a few weak hours of sun. The family has to choose: move south, or watch you starve.
- **Real facts:** noon sun delivers about 1,000 watts per m²; plants turn only ~1–2 % of it into food. With ~1 m² of
  you facing the sun, you'd need roughly 25–50 m² of green skin to eat a day's worth *(check: our own math)*. The sea
  slug *Elysia chlorotica* steals chloroplasts from algae and runs on sunlight for months. Spotted salamander eggs
  have algae living inside their cells. Plants are green because they throw the green light away. Astronauts ate the
  first lettuce grown on the International Space Station in 2015, under red and blue lamps.
- **Hero / world-scale:** scientists want to copy your green cells for everyone who goes hungry.
- **Twist:** first day of spring. Something itches behind your ear. It's a leaf.
- **Comment question:** What food would you miss first?

## D. What If You Could Hear Every Lie?
**Rule:** every lie said out loud near you makes a buzz only you can hear. Even the little ones. Even yours.
*(The loaded gun: "even yours".)*
**Family:** Grandpa, who taught you poker and never once let you win.

Hook:
1. You show Mom your drawing.
2. "I love it, sweetie."
3. BZZZT. Like a wrong answer on a game show, right in your ear.
4. You look around. Nobody else heard it.
5. Your friend calls. "I'm five minutes away." BZZZT.
6. Dad, at dinner: "I'm not tired at all." BZZZT.
7. By bedtime, you've heard it 14 times.
8. So, what if you could hear every lie?

- **Fun:** you catch the snack thief; "the dog ate my homework" (the teacher's face); poker night against Grandpa;
  the dentist: "this won't hurt a bit".
- **Pivot:** "Then you start counting."
- **Curse ladder:** comic: TV ads are a wall of buzzing → social: your best friend's kind lies ("nice haircut"), you
  call them out, people stop talking to you → danger / hero (middle): at the park a man tells a little girl "your mom
  sent me to get you." BZZZT. You yell. He runs → emotional: Grandpa in the hospital: "I'm not scared." BZZZT. Dad:
  "He'll be fine." BZZZT. Grandpa asks, "Are you scared?" You say "No." The loudest buzz of your life is your own.
  You hold his hand anyway, and he smiles, because he knows.
- **Real facts:** people tell about 1–2 lies a day (a 1996 diary study). In a 2002 UMass study, 60 % of people lied
  at least once in a 10-minute chat with a stranger. Across 206 studies, people catch lies 54 % of the time, barely
  better than a coin flip. People in 58 countries believe liars look away; they don't. A US National Academy of
  Sciences report (2003) found polygraphs measure stress, not lies. Many everyday lies are told to make someone else
  feel better.
- **Twist:** walking home, you whisper, "Nobody else can hear this." BZZZT. Across the street, a girl stops, turns,
  and smiles at you.
- **Comment question:** Which lie would you never want to hear?

## E. What If You Could Talk to Animals?
**Rule:** you understand every animal and they understand you, and you can't switch it off. *(The loaded gun: they
never stop talking, and they don't lie to make you feel better.)*
**Family:** Biscuit, the family dog, 14, older than your little sister.

Hook:
1. Breakfast. Biscuit stares at you, like every morning.
2. Except this time, you hear him.
3. "Bacon. Under the chair. Left side."
4. You look. There's bacon under the chair. Left side.
5. "That's forty-one pieces this year. I've been counting."
6. The cat walks past: "Ugh. You're awake."
7. Outside, a bird screams the same word, over and over: "MINE! MINE! MINE!"
8. So, what if you could talk to animals?

- **Fun:** ask Biscuit what he wants for his birthday; ask the cat why she pushes cups off the table; pigeons give
  directions; the crows start bringing you gifts; prairie dogs describe you to each other ("tall one, yellow shirt").
- **Pivot:** "Then the birds wake you up at 4:47 a.m."
- **Curse ladder:** comic: the dawn chorus is every bird in town yelling "mine" → danger: you insult one crow, and
  now every crow in the neighbourhood dives at you, and they teach their chicks your face → social: the school
  cafeteria serves chicken nuggets; the pet store is a room of animals begging "take me home" → emotional: Biscuit
  tells you his hips hurt and he's tired. He asks you to sit with him. He tells you not to be sad. You hear every
  word of goodbye.
- **Real facts:** prairie dog alarm calls describe a person's size and shirt colour (Con Slobodchikoff). Honeybees'
  waggle dance gives direction and distance (Karl von Frisch, Nobel Prize 1973). In 2024, scientists found wild
  African elephants call each other by individual names. Crows remember a human face for years and teach other crows
  to scold it (John Marzluff, University of Washington). A Border Collie named Chaser learned the names of 1,022
  toys. Rats laugh when you tickle them. Project CETI is using AI to decode sperm whale clicks.
- **World-scale:** Project CETI asks you to translate the first message from the whales.
- **Twist:** that night, from the sea, thousands of whales repeat one word. Your name.
- **Comment question:** Which animal would you talk to first, and what would you ask?

**Pick for our channel:** A (Human Magnet). It's the most visual in the 2D studio (things flying at the hero every
few seconds), it has world-scale effects (robins, magnetic north) and an emotional price (Leo), and it makes an easy
close-up thumbnail. D (Every Lie) is the cheapest to animate and the most emotional.
