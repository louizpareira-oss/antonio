## A. Episode brief

| | |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Seventy Percent Off What? |
| **Idea** | A store once stopped faking its sales — and its shoppers punished it. The "savings" were a feeling all along. |
| **Hook line** | "Seventy percent off! Never pay full price!" |
| **The turn** | "But shoppers hate it." — the honest store fails |
| **Payoff line** | "Next time you see a slashed price, ask who wrote the first number." |
| **Signature object** | The red sale tag. In the black-and-white proof it is the only colour: it drains out of the store while the store is honest and floods back when the sales return. |
| **Counterpart** | Hazel, a hedgehog neighbour and proud bargain hunter |
| **Why Hazel** | Her identity is "I never pay full price", so the twist lands on exactly the viewer who thinks they are too smart for tricks. Small and round against tall, slim Reynard; the neighbour relationship puts the story in her kitchen. |
| **Wants** | To feel like a smart shopper |
| **Belief** | A big discount means real savings |
| **Discovery** | The "original" price is a story, and a store that dropped it was abandoned by its shoppers |
| **Visible choice** | She folds the dress back into the bag with the receipt on top — it is going back |

**Facts (checked):**
- JCPenney hired a new CEO, Ron Johnson, in 2011.
- In 2012 he replaced its coupon-and-sale pricing with "fair and square" everyday prices, calling the old ones "fake prices".
- Revenue fell from about $17.3 billion (fiscal 2011) to about $13 billion (fiscal 2012): roughly a quarter, over $4 billion.
- He was fired on April 8, 2013, and the sales and coupons came back.
- Hazel's $100 dress is fiction.

Business writers name several causes for the fall, including a store redesign and losing loyal shoppers. The script never says pricing was the only one — only that shoppers hated it and stayed away.
