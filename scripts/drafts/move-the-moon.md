## The rule of the story
- Close your hand around the Moon in the sky, and you can pull it closer, push it away, or turn it.
- **Whatever you do to the Moon, the Moon does back to you.** (Newton's third law, as a story rule.)

## The voiceover script
Speaker tags: unmarked lines are the NARRATOR. `DAD:` is Dad.

```
[ACT 1 — HOOK]
Can one person really move the Moon?
Not in a movie. With real physics.
Tonight, the Moon is sitting in your hand.
The real one. You close your fingers, and up in the sky, it moves.
Here's the rule. You can pull it closer. Push it away. Even turn it around.
But whatever you do to the Moon, the Moon does back to you.
Remember that.

[ACT 1 — DAD]
Your dad is a fisherman. He reads the tide the way other people read the time.
DAD: High tide at nine. Never argue with the Moon.
Twenty-eight years ago, on a full moon, his father took the Marigold out.
The boat came back. Grandpa didn't.
Dad hasn't sailed on a full moon since.

[ACT 2 — THE FUN PART]
Day one. You pull the Moon a little closer. The whole town comes outside to take pictures.
Day two. You push it back, the tide runs out, and you walk to the island. Dry shoes.
Science fair. You slide the Moon in front of the Sun, at noon, on cue. First place.
Then you turn it around.
For the first time ever, the whole world sees the far side of the Moon. Live.
You make it your wallpaper.

[ACT 3 — THE CURSE]
But the Moon does back to you.
Pull the Moon ten percent closer, and its pull on the oceans gets more than a third stronger.
The harbour floods. Then coasts all over the world.
Corals that spawn by the light of the full moon spawn on the wrong night.
Laser observatories say the Moon is kilometres out of place.
NASA calls it the drift.
And every time you pull, something pulls back. You wake up facing the window. Every night.
You can't turn your back on the Moon anymore.

[ACT 4 — IS ANY OF THIS REAL?]
So. Is any of this real?
You already move the Moon. Your gravity tugs on it right now, with about the weight of a few grains of rice.
The Moon is drifting away, three point eight centimetres a year. Apollo astronauts left mirrors up there, and we still bounce lasers off them.
As it drifts, it slows the Earth. Every century, the day gets about two thousandths of a second longer.
In 2022, NASA crashed a spacecraft into a tiny asteroid moon and changed its orbit by thirty-two minutes.
Pushing our Moon away for good? You'd need all of humanity's energy for about sixty million years.
And the Moon really is stuck facing us. Scientists call it tidal locking.
Every piece is real. Just never in one hand.

[ACT 5 — THE KING TIDE]
Then comes the full moon. The highest tide of the year.
A storm pushes a boat toward the rocks.
DAD: That's Lena's boat. Her kids are on it.
And Dad sails on a full moon.
You push the Moon away. Harder than you ever have.
The tide drops. The sea lets go of the boats. Dad comes home.
And the Moon pushes back. One last time.
Your body locks. Forever. Wherever the Moon goes, you turn to face it.
Dad turns your bed toward the window.
DAD: Now you know why I never argue with it.

[ACT 5 — THE TWIST]
Then he takes Grandpa's photo off the fridge.
DAD: The night he vanished, the tide dropped in a minute. Nobody could explain it.
DAD: He used to sleep facing the window too.
That night, the Moon moves again.
Your hand is down.
Someone else is pulling.

[END]
If you could move the Moon just once, where would you put it?
Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because someone else is pulling.
```

## Fact notes (to check before voicing)
- **Your pull on the Moon:** F = G·m·M / r² with m = 70 kg, M = 7.35 × 10²² kg, r = 384,400 km gives about 2 × 10⁻³ N,
  the weight of roughly a quarter of a gram (a few grains of rice). Newton's third law: the Moon pulls you exactly as
  hard.
- **Tides vs distance:** the tidal force scales with 1/d³, so a Moon 10 % closer pulls about 37 % harder on the oceans
  (0.9⁻³ ≈ 1.37). Real tide heights also depend on coastlines and resonance; "more than a third stronger" refers to
  the pull, not the height.
- **Lunar laser ranging:** Apollo 11, 14 and 15 left retroreflectors (two Soviet Lunokhod rovers carry more);
  observatories such as Apache Point (New Mexico) and Grasse (France) still bounce lasers off them and measure the
  Earth–Moon distance to about a millimetre. The Moon recedes about 3.8 cm a year.
- **Longer days:** tidal friction transfers Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit; tides lengthen the day by about
  2.3 ms per century (the net measured rate is about 1.7–1.8 ms per century). "About two thousandths of a second" holds.
- **DART:** on Sept 26, 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft hit Dimorphos, the small moon of the asteroid Didymos, and
  shortened its 11 h 55 min orbit by about 32 minutes (later refined to about 33 ± 1 min). "Thirty-two minutes" is the
  first figure NASA announced.
- **Energy to free the Moon:** the Moon's orbital energy is about GMm / 2a ≈ 3.8 × 10²⁸ J; world primary energy use is
  about 6 × 10²⁰ J a year (~600 EJ), so ≈ 6 × 10⁷ years: "about sixty million years".
- **Far side:** the Moon is tidally locked; the far side was first photographed by Luna 3 in 1959 and has never been
  visible from Earth.
- **Coral spawning:** many reef corals (e.g. on the Great Barrier Reef) time their mass spawning to the lunar cycle,
  a few nights after a full moon.
- **Story, not fact:** grabbing the Moon, moving it for an eclipse or to show the far side, the floods, NASA's "drift",
  the hero turning to face the Moon, the tide dropping in a minute in 1998, someone else pulling.
