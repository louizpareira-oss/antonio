## The rule of the story
- You can breathe underwater. Any water. Salt, fresh, a bathtub, rain.
- **The water lets you breathe, but water never gives anything back for free.** Every hour under the surface, the air
  up here gets a little harder to breathe.

## The voiceover script
Speaker tags: unmarked lines are the NARRATOR. `GRANDPA:` is Grandpa Ilan. `MAYA:` is your sister.

```
[ACT 1 — HOOK]
Can one person really breathe underwater?
Not in a cartoon. With real science. Let's find out.
Right now, you're at the bottom of the public pool. Eyes open. Mouth open.
And no bubbles. None.
You've been down here eleven minutes, and you feel fine.
It started this morning in the shower, when you took a breath with your face in the water. And it worked.
Here's the rule. The water lets you breathe. But water never gives anything back for free.
Remember that.

[ACT 1 — GRANDPA]
Grandpa Ilan was a Navy diver for thirty years.
GRANDPA: Three rules, kid. Never hold your breath. Never dive alone. And never trust the sea when it's being nice to you.
His dive watch stopped at three forty. His last dive. He came up too fast, and his left ear never worked again.
He still sleeps with the window open, so he can hear the water.

[ACT 2 — THE FUN PART]
Day one. You do your homework at the bottom of the pool, because nobody can bother you there.
Day two. The aquarium. You sit in the shark tank, cross-legged, and wave at a school group.
Day three. The Coast Guard is looking for a sunken fishing boat. You find it in twenty minutes and bring up the logbook.
You're on the news. No tanks. No mask. Just a kid in a wet hoodie.
And when the river floods, you walk in, and you walk a stuck dog out.
Maya stands on the bank in her raincoat, clapping.
MAYA: Do it again!

[ACT 3 — THE CURSE]
But water never gives anything back for free.
Every hour under the surface, the air up here gets harder. Thinner. Like a mountain that keeps growing.
By Friday, walking up the stairs burns. Your lips go blue in gym class.
A doctor checks your blood. It's thick. Your body is building red cells like you live on Everest.
Then the sea starts noticing you.
A pod of humpback whales follows you along the coast. Then a second pod.
NOAA spots a cold spot in the ocean. It moves. It moves exactly where you swim.
And every night, the tide comes a little higher up the street where you sleep. Just a few centimetres.
That's enough. By Sunday, the harbour is in the parking lot.
The Coast Guard calls it a seiche.
It's you.

[ACT 4 — IS ANY OF THIS REAL?]
So. Is any of this real?
Here's the problem. A litre of air holds about two hundred and ten millilitres of oxygen. A litre of seawater holds about seven.
To get enough oxygen, you'd have to pump more water through your chest every minute than a fire hose.
But in 1966, a scientist named Leland Clark put a mouse into a liquid full of oxygen. It breathed. And it lived.
And the Bajau people of Southeast Asia dive for up to ten minutes. Their spleens are fifty percent bigger than their neighbours'.
Real people. Real evolution. Just slower than a shower.
And Grandpa's ear? That's real too. Come up too fast, and the nitrogen in your blood turns into bubbles. Divers call it the bends.

[ACT 5 — GRANDPA]
One night, Grandpa isn't in his bed. His boots are on the dock.
Three forty, on his watch. The same dive. Sixty metres down, in the dark, is the wreck where he lost his hearing.
You go down. No light. No tank. Just your hands.
And you find him, sitting on the deck of the wreck, holding his breath, like he always told you not to.
You give him your air. Mouth to mouth. Water into lungs that were built for it, for one minute.
He wakes up on the sand, coughing.
GRANDPA: You broke all three rules, kid.
And you realise you can't breathe. Not the air. The air up here is gone for you.
That's what the water took.
You can go home for ten minutes at a time. Then you have to go back in.

[ACT 5 — THE TWIST]
Then, the next morning, Maya walks to the shore in her raincoat.
She puts her face in the water. And she doesn't come up.
No bubbles.
GRANDPA: It's not you. It was never just you. Something in the water is choosing.
Out past the harbour, the cold spot turns. And it starts coming in.

[END]
If you could only breathe in the water, or only in the air, which one would you keep?
Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because Maya is already down there.
```

## Fact notes (to check before voicing)
- **Oxygen in air vs water:** air is ~21 % O₂ by volume, so ~210 mL per litre; air-saturated seawater at ~15 °C holds
  roughly 6–8 mL O₂ per litre. A human at rest needs ~250 mL O₂/min, so you'd need tens of litres of water per minute
  through a gill at realistic extraction; fish manage it because they are cold-blooded and need far less.
- **Liquid breathing:** Leland C. Clark Jr. and Frank Gollan, *Science*, 1966: mice survived submerged in oxygenated
  silicone oil and perfluorocarbon liquids. Perfluorocarbon liquid ventilation has been tested in human trials.
- **Bajau divers:** Ilardo et al., *Cell*, 2018: Bajau people have spleens ~50 % larger than neighbouring Saluan,
  linked to the PDE10A gene; dives of several minutes, reported up to ~10+ minutes.
- **The bends (decompression sickness):** nitrogen dissolved under pressure forms bubbles on fast ascent; inner-ear
  decompression sickness can cause permanent hearing loss.
- **Polycythemia at altitude:** the body makes more red blood cells in low oxygen; chronic cases thicken the blood.
- **Seiche:** a real standing wave in harbours and lakes (NOAA term), not caused by a person.
- **Story, not fact:** breathing water, the air "going thin", whales and a cold spot following a person, the tide
  rising at your street, Maya.
