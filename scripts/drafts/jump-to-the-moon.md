## The rule of the story
- Your legs can push with any force you want. That's the only superpower.
- **Every jump down is the same jump up.** Whatever you put into the ground, the ground puts back into you, and
  whatever you leave from, you land on just as hard.

## The voiceover script
Speaker tags: unmarked lines are the NARRATOR. `DAD:` is Dad; `MAYA:` is Maya.

```
[ACT 1 — HOOK]
Can one person really jump to the Moon?
Not in a cartoon. With real physics. Let's find out.
Right now, your left sneaker is above the clouds.
You can see the curve of the Earth under it. And you are still going up.
This morning, you tried to jump over a puddle.
You landed in the next town.
Here's the rule. Your legs can push as hard as you want.
But every jump down is the same jump up. Whatever you give the ground, the ground gives back.
Remember that.

[ACT 1 — DAD]
Dad was a long-jumper. Eight metres, once. He has a medal and a knee that clicks.
DAD: It's not the legs. It's the landing. Nobody trains the landing.
He blows the whistle every morning at six. You hate the whistle.
He never jumped again after the knee. But he still measures your puddle jumps with a tape.

[ACT 2 — THE FUN PART]
Day one. You jump over the school. The flag on the roof waves at you.
Day two. The whole city. Maya films it from the bus.
MAYA: He's a dot. He's a DOT.
One kilometre. Ten. You wave at a window. There's a plane behind the window.
At thirty kilometres the sky turns black at noon, and the stars come out while the sun is still up.
Every jump, the backyard gets a little lower. Dad calls it the crater.
Your hoodie smells like a campfire.

[ACT 3 — THE CURSE]
But every jump down is the same jump up.
To leave the ground at ten kilometres a second, you have to push on it that hard. The push cracks the street.
Windows break for three blocks. Then ten.
You go faster than sound, and the boom follows you across the town like a slap.
At thirty kilometres a second, you are a shooting star. Going the wrong way.
The air in front of you turns into fire. The hoodie goes black at the sleeves.
And then the air runs out. You can't breathe at thirty kilometres. You can't scream at forty.
NASA puts a red dot on your backyard. They're tracking you like a meteor.
You are the meteor.

[ACT 4 — IS ANY OF THIS REAL?]
So. Is any of this real?
There is a speed that lets you leave Earth and never come back. Eleven point two kilometres a second. Scientists call it escape velocity.
A real rocket never jumps. It pushes for nine minutes, and pulls three times your weight the whole way.
Leave the ground at escape speed in one push, and the push itself turns you into paste.
Space starts at one hundred kilometres. The Kármán line.
The Moon is three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres further.
Apollo took three days to get there. And the Apollo astronauts weighed one sixth of normal when they landed.
Which is why a Moon jump is easy. It's the Earth jump that kills you.

[ACT 5 — DAD]
One night, you decide to do it. The whole way.
Dad is in the crater with the tape. He doesn't say don't.
DAD: It's not the legs. It's the landing. Tuck. Roll. Breathe out before you hit.
You push. The backyard drops ten metres. The whole street tilts.
Three days of silence. The hoodie freezes solid. The Earth gets small enough to cover with your thumb.
And you land. One sixth of your weight. Soft as a mattress.
You stand on the Moon in a frosted hoodie, and you can see the whole Earth. Every lie you ever told is on it.
Here's the price. Every jump down is the same jump up.
To jump home, you have to push on the Moon. And the Moon pushes back.
You land in the crater. Dad is standing there. Older. Much older.
DAD: Three days up there. Six months down here. The crater's a lake now.
Your pushes didn't go nowhere. They went into the ground.

[ACT 5 — THE TWIST]
That night, ESA posts a new chart. The Moon's orbit has changed. A few centimetres.
Your push moved it.
And it's not drifting away. It's coming closer.
Dad blows the whistle. He points at the Moon.
There's a red dot on it. It's not yours.
Someone else is jumping. Towards us.

[END]
If you could make one jump, and only one, where would you land?
Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because the next one lands here.
```

## Fact notes (to check before voicing)
- **Escape velocity** at Earth's surface: about 11.2 km/s. Leaving at that speed in a single instant push is a story
  device; the instantaneous acceleration would be lethal (any push to km/s over a metre or so is thousands of g).
- **Rocket launch:** Saturn V / Space Shuttle style ascents last roughly 8–9 minutes to orbit, peaking around 3 g
  (the Shuttle was throttled to 3 g; Saturn V first stage reached ~4 g at cut-off). "Three times your weight the whole
  way" is a rounding; say "up to three times" if the user prefers exactness.
- **Kármán line:** 100 km, the usual boundary of space (FAI).
- **Moon distance:** mean 384,400 km. Apollo 11: launch Jul 16, lunar landing Jul 20, 1969 (~3 days to lunar orbit).
- **Lunar gravity:** about 1/6 of Earth's (1.62 m/s²).
- **Air:** at 30 km the pressure is ~1 % of sea level; the "Armstrong limit" where body fluids boil is ~19 km.
  Nobody can breathe unaided much above ~8 km.
- **Sonic boom:** anything faster than ~343 m/s at sea level makes one; meteors enter at 11–72 km/s and glow from
  air compression ahead of them (not friction, strictly).
- **Time:** 3 days up vs 6 months down is **story, not fact**; there is no real time dilation at these speeds. Keep it
  as the lasting price, or swap for a real price (Dad's health, the crater, the town) if the user wants it strictly real.
- **Moon's orbit:** the Moon actually recedes ~3.8 cm a year (laser ranging). A human push moving it is **story**.
- **Story, not fact:** the super-legs, the crater, the red dots, the other jumper.
