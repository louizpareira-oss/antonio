## The voiceover script

```
You step back from the curb.
One second later, a bike flies past, exactly where you were standing.
The rider shouts sorry. Your best friend, Mia, stares at you.
"How did you know?"
You didn't hear it. You didn't see it coming.
You saw it ten seconds ago.

What if you could see ten seconds into the future?

It starts on a Tuesday. You blink, and for a moment there are two worlds on top of each other.
The real one... and a faint blue copy, running ten seconds ahead.
In the blue world, your phone slides off the table. In the real world, it hasn't moved yet.
You wait. Seven. Eight. Nine.
It slides. You catch it without looking.
The rules are simple. You only see what your own eyes are going to see. Never more than ten seconds ahead. And it never switches off.

At first, it's the best thing that has ever happened to you.
You never step in a puddle again. You never walk into a glass door.
Rock, paper, scissors? You've won four hundred games in a row. Nobody plays with you anymore.
In class, you know the answer before the teacher finishes the question, because you already heard it... from the kid who's about to get it wrong.
And then there are penalty kicks.
The striker runs up. To everyone else, it's a guess. To you, it's a rerun. You already watched the ball go bottom left.
You dive bottom left. Every. Single. Time.

But living ten seconds ahead has a price.
Every joke reaches you twice. First the punchline, then the setup. You laugh at the wrong moment, and people stop telling you jokes.
Conversations feel like reruns. You answer questions nobody has asked yet, and your friends start to find it creepy.
Surprise parties don't exist for you anymore.
And your brain is running two movies at the same time. By evening, the headaches are so bad that you sit in a dark room with your eyes closed, just to make one of the worlds go away.

Then comes the part nobody warns you about.
At a restaurant, you see a waiter across the room trip, and a tray of glasses shatter. You stand up. You start to move.
Ten seconds is long enough to see it.
It isn't always long enough to stop it.
The glasses break. Exactly the way you saw them break.
And from that day on, every accident you see in the blue world feels like a question. Are you fast enough?

Friday, four twelve p.m. You and Mia are walking home.
She has her earbuds in. She's looking at her phone. She steps toward the crosswalk.
And in the blue world, ten seconds ahead, you see a truck run the red light.
Ten.
You shout her name. She can't hear you.
Nine. Eight.
You're thirty meters behind her. You run.
Seven. And the blue world changes. Because you moved, the future moved too. Now the truck isn't just heading for Mia. It's heading for both of you.
Six. Five.
For the first time, your power isn't telling you what will happen. It's asking you what you're willing to do.
Four. Three.
You don't stop.
Two.
You grab her backpack and pull. You both fall hard onto the sidewalk.
One.
The truck roars through the space where she was standing.
Zero.

Mia stares at you, shaking. "How did you know?"
And for once, you tell her the truth. "I didn't know. I saw."

So, is any of this real?
Not the blue world. But part of it is happening inside your head right now.
Your brain is always about a tenth of a second behind reality. That's how long it takes to process what your eyes see.
To hide that delay, your brain quietly predicts where moving things are about to be.
A fastball reaches the batter in about four tenths of a second. Batters don't swing at where the ball is. They swing at where their brain predicts it will be.
So in a tiny way, you already live a little bit in the future.
You'd just be living there a hundred times further ahead.

That night, you lie in bed and close your eyes.
And for the first time in your life, the blue world shows you... nothing.
The next ten seconds are completely black.

What would YOU do with ten seconds of the future? Tell us in the comments.
```

## Fact notes (for the description)
- Visual perception lags reality by roughly 0.1 s; the brain compensates by predicting motion (flash-lag effect).
- A 95 mph fastball covers the 60.5 ft from mound to plate in about 0.43 s.
- 10 s ÷ 0.1 s = 100× further ahead than the brain's own prediction.
